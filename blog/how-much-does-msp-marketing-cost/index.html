How Much Does MSP Marketing Cost? A Real 2026 Breakdown

Ask five MSP owners what they spend on marketing and you’ll get five different answers, and most of them will only be counting the agency invoice.

That’s the first problem, and it matters because winning new clients is the thing MSPs say they struggle with most. In Kaseya’s 2026 State of the MSP report, customer acquisition came out as the top priority and the biggest obstacle, with 71 percent of MSPs naming it their biggest challenge.

Marketing for an MSP can cost anywhere from $500 to $20,000 a month depending on whether you’re doing it yourself, hiring someone in-house, paying an agency, or bringing in a fractional CMO. That range is wide on purpose.

A $1.2M MSP buying blog posts and basic SEO is playing a completely different game than a $7M MSP running paid search, LinkedIn, AI search, and a full content engine.

So the useful question isn’t “how much does MSP marketing cost?” It’s “how much does the version of marketing that actually produces pipeline cost for an MSP my size?” Those are two different questions, and the second one is the one worth answering. Here’s the full picture, including the costs that never make it into a proposal.

What MSP Marketing Actually Costs Per Month

Agency retainers for MSPs run from roughly $500 to $20,000 a month. Most full-service programs start near $3,000, and the common band for an MSP under $5M in revenue is $2,500 to $4,000.

That’s the agency line item, and it’s only part of the number. Here’s the rough shape of each model:

  • DIY or content-only: Basic SEO, blog posts, and social, where you’re doing most of the work yourself. Best for smaller MSPs building a content floor before they can justify a real budget.
  • Single-channel agency: One channel done well, usually SEO or Google Ads. Good for testing whether one channel can produce leads before you commit more.
  • Full-service agency: Multi-channel execution with an account manager and monthly reports. This is where most MSPs with real revenue end up.
  • Leadership plus execution: A fractional CMO owns the strategy while a team runs the channels underneath it. Foundation work like positioning and your ideal client is included, and marketing is tied to pipeline.
  • Full in-house team: A marketing manager, tools, and ad spend. Direct control, but you need enough volume to keep someone busy.

Two things to watch for. First, ad spend is almost never included in an agency retainer.

A $5,000 retainer that manages your Google Ads means $5,000 for management plus whatever you put into the platform. Ask which number you’re looking at before you sign anything.

Second, the model you need isn’t decided by what you can afford this month. It’s decided by what one new client is worth to you over five years, and that math changes the whole conversation.

The Most Expensive Marketing Is the Kind With No Strategy

This is the cost that never shows up as a line item. It shows up as twelve months of retainer payments, ad spend, and content that never connected to a single closed deal.

Here’s what that looks like. A $4,000 monthly retainer running for a year with nothing measurable coming out of it is $48,000.

Add $2,000 a month in Google Ads spend running without clean conversion tracking, and that’s another $24,000. Toss in tools and your own time and you’re past $80,000 for a year of activity with nothing to show for it.

That’s the number that makes owners say “marketing doesn’t work for MSPs.” The channels worked fine. Nobody was connecting them to revenue.

And the acquisition cost on its own isn’t the scary part. In Robin Robins’ breakdown of MSP customer acquisition costs, best-in-class MSPs spend more than $27,000 per new customer once staff, CRM, and website costs are included, and the average new client brings in roughly $2,200 to $2,500 a month.

At $2,200 a month, that’s $132,000 over a five-year relationship. Spending $27,000 to earn $132,000 is a trade almost any business owner would take.

It’s also a reminder to pay attention to client size. The same article points out that MSPs whose clients spend under $2,500 a month may struggle to afford paid ads at all, because the math only works when each client is worth enough.

The problem is never the acquisition cost. The problem is paying it and getting nothing back.

What Each Marketing Channel Costs

Channel costs swing a lot depending on competition and geography, but here’s how to think about each one:

SEO and content. This is a slow compounder. Expect six to twelve months before it really pays off, and a monthly cost that scales with how much content and technical work you need.

It’s where most MSPs should be investing alongside paid. You can see how we approach it on our SEO services page.

Google Ads. Results are fastest here, with useful data in 30 to 60 days. Google Ads tends to work well for MSPs spending roughly $2,000 to $4,000 a month in ad spend, and that’s the platform spend alone, before any management fee, which usually runs 10 to 20 percent.

Unmanaged campaigns burn budget fast, so this channel needs weekly attention. Our walkthrough on how to run Google Ads for your MSP covers the setup, and the broader service is on our Google Ads and PPC page.

LinkedIn. Organic is free in cash and costs you a few hours a week, and for most MSPs it’s the higher-return play. Paid LinkedIn gets expensive quickly, since clicks cost more than on most other channels, so you want tight targeting and a strong offer before you spend.

I cover the full approach in the MSP LinkedIn ads strategy.

Local SEO and your Google Business Profile. One of the cheapest places to start. Profile optimization, citations, and a review strategy can move you in the map pack within 30 to 90 days.

Email. Low cost, and it compounds as your list grows. It’s the cheapest way to stay in front of people who already know you.

AI search optimization. This is a newer line item for 2026. It means restructuring your content so AI tools can extract and cite it, and most MSPs haven’t started yet.

That’s a window, not a permanent advantage.

In-House vs. Agency vs. Fractional CMO

An in-house marketing manager runs roughly $110,000 to $155,000 a year once you add salary, benefits, tools, and management time. An agency runs about $3,000 to $8,000 a month.

A fractional CMO runs about $3,000 to $15,000 a month. They each solve a different problem, and a lot of MSPs compare them as if they don’t.

An agency executes channels. An in-house hire handles day-to-day marketing tasks.

Neither one, by default, owns the strategy that decides which channels to run, what your positioning is, and how marketing connects to your sales pipeline. That’s the layer a fractional CMO provides, and it’s the one most MSPs skip because it feels like a luxury.

It isn’t. It’s the difference between spending $4,000 a month on campaigns tied to revenue and spending $4,000 a month on campaigns that just generate reports.

For MSPs under about $5M in revenue, a full in-house hire rarely makes financial sense yet. In my 3-year growth roadmap, the first marketing hire is a coordinator who executes a plan that’s already working, which only happens once the plan exists.

The Tool Costs Nobody Puts in the Proposal

Your agency retainer doesn’t include your CRM, your SEO tools, your email platform, or your analytics. Those are your costs, every month, whether the marketing is working or not.

A realistic stack for an MSP looks like this:

  • CRM and marketing automation: Most MSPs land between $100 and $500 a month, though HubSpot can climb well past that once you need real automation
  • SEO and research tools: $100 to $500 a month
  • Email platform: $30 to $300 a month, depending on list size
  • Social scheduling: $30 to $100 a month
  • Call and form tracking: $50 to $200 a month
  • AI and productivity tools: $20 to $200 a month
  • Website hosting and maintenance: $50 to $300 a month

All in, that’s $300 to $1,500 a month. It’s rarely in the agency proposal, so you’re budgeting for the car but not the gas.

Your website matters here too, since it’s what every channel sends traffic to, which is why our web design work is built around conversion, not just looks.

Is Your MSP Marketing Spend Actually Working?

One number justifies marketing spend: what it costs to acquire a client compared to what that client is worth over their lifetime. Everything else is a supporting metric.

The simple version is to take your average monthly recurring revenue per client, multiply by the number of months they stay, then multiply by your profit margin. That’s your client lifetime value.

Keep your acquisition cost at or below about 20 percent of that number.

Here’s an example. A client paying $3,500 a month who stays for five years at a 30 percent margin is worth $63,000.

A max defensible acquisition cost of 20 percent is $12,600. So spending $5,000 to $8,000 across sales and marketing to land that client isn’t aggressive.

It’s just math.

If an MSP owner tells me marketing is too expensive, it’s almost always one of two things. Either they don’t know their lifetime value, so any number feels high, or they’ve been spending on marketing that never connected to revenue, so the return comes back blank.

A quick way to tell where you stand:

  • You know your cost per qualified lead and it’s trending down: It’s working, so keep going
  • You know the cost and it’s too high: Your positioning, targeting, or channel mix needs adjusting, which is a strategy decision, not a spending decision
  • You don’t know your cost per qualified lead at all: You have a measurement problem before you have a budget problem, so fix that first

Vendor Marketing Money You’re Probably Leaving on the Table

Here’s one that a lot of MSPs miss. If you’re a Microsoft, Kaseya, ConnectWise, or other major vendor partner, there’s often co-op marketing money (usually called MDF) available that can offset what you spend on campaigns.

The amounts vary a lot by vendor and partner tier, and the rules are specific. Microsoft publishes its co-op funds resources for partners, and MSP Success’s breakdown of Kaseya’s MDF program shows how restrictive it can be. For example, it covers things like webinars, events, and in-house digital campaigns, but excludes third-party marketing agencies, and typical amounts there run from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars.

So don’t count on it covering an agency retainer. But it’s free money for the activities it does cover, so ask your vendor reps what’s available and what it can be spent on.

Nobody is going to remind you to claim it.

What You Should Actually Budget

You’ve probably heard the advice to spend a flat percentage of revenue on marketing. It’s not wrong, but it doesn’t tell you where to put the money or what to expect back.

The data is more useful than the rule of thumb. ConnectWise’s 2026 MSP Marketing Report, citing Service Leadership data, puts the best-in-class marketing budget for MSPs at 1.8 percent of revenue. Most MSPs sit well below that, and the same report’s survey found that 51 percent of respondents spend under $10,000 a year on marketing.

On a $4M MSP, 1.8 percent works out to $6,000 a month. At 1 percent, it’s $3,333 a month.

That’s a difference of about $2,667 a month. Not a massive jump, but enough to add a real channel or bring in strategic leadership, and enough to move from activity to outcomes.

A good way to scale it by stage:

  • Smaller MSPs: Put the money into content infrastructure, local SEO, and your Google Business Profile. One or two channels, done properly
  • Mid-sized MSPs: Add strategic SEO and content, LinkedIn, and a small paid budget to validate what works, along with real work on positioning
  • Larger MSPs: Run multiple channels together with senior leadership, tie everything to pipeline, and consider an in-house hire

Where This Leaves You

MSP marketing cost isn’t one number. It’s a stack of decisions about which model to use, which channels to run, what tools to pay for, and whether anyone owns the strategy tying it together.

Three things worth taking away:

  • Know the full cost, not just the retainer. Agency fees, ad spend, tools, and your own time all count
  • Match your investment to your stage. Underspending when you’re ready to scale is as wasteful as overspending too early
  • Measure against lifetime value, not gut feel. If a client is worth $100,000 or more over time, the question isn’t whether $5,000 of marketing is expensive. It’s whether that $5,000 produced a qualified conversation

FAQ

How much should an MSP spend on marketing per month?

It depends on your size and goals, but ConnectWise’s 2026 MSP Marketing Report cites 1.8 percent of revenue as the best-in-class benchmark. In dollar terms, most MSPs under $5M in revenue land somewhere between $2,500 and $4,000 a month on an agency, plus ad spend and tools.

Is ad spend included in agency pricing?

Almost never. Retainers cover management.

The money you put into Google or LinkedIn is on top, so always ask which number you’re being quoted.

How long before MSP marketing generates a return?

Paid search can give you usable data in 30 to 60 days. SEO and content usually take six to twelve months to compound, and LinkedIn typically takes a few months of consistent effort.

What’s the cost difference between an agency and a fractional CMO?

Agencies typically run $3,000 to $8,000 a month and execute channels. A fractional CMO runs about $3,000 to $15,000 a month and owns the strategy.

Many MSPs need both layers eventually.

Should a smaller MSP spend differently than a bigger one?

Yes. Smaller MSPs should focus on one or two channels, usually local SEO, content, and referrals.

Larger MSPs can afford to run several channels together with strategic leadership on top.
