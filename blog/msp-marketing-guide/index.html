71% of MSPs say getting new clients is their single biggest business challenge. That number comes from Kaseya’s 2026 State of the MSP Report, a survey of 1,061 MSPs run in November 2025.

It sits well above cybersecurity (53%) and revenue growth (49%) on the same list.

The same survey found that a third of MSPs now win most of their new clients by taking them from another provider. Only 12% mainly sign businesses that are outsourcing IT for the first time.

So demand for managed services isn’t going anywhere. What changed is who wins it.

Private equity platforms buy local providers and sell “local service, national scale.” Vendors ship AI tooling that squeezes the tier-1 helpdesk margin most MSPs used to live on.

And the referral network that took you from zero to a million in revenue has a ceiling. It shows up right when you want to stop selling.

This guide covers MSP marketing as it works going into 2027. We’ll look at why it’s harder now, how to position your MSP so you stop competing on price, and which channels produce real leads versus just activity.

Then we’ll get into how much to spend, how to build a plan for your revenue stage, how the US and Europe differ, what AI changes, and the mistakes I see most often.

It’s written for MSP owners and for whoever you hire to run your marketing, whether that’s someone in-house or an agency.

Quick Answer: What Is MSP Marketing?

MSP marketing is how a managed service provider becomes the IT partner a business already trusts before it starts looking for one, and then turns that trust into recurring revenue.

It covers positioning, local SEO, content, LinkedIn, referrals, paid ads, and email. And you measure all of it against pipeline, not activity.

One quick note: here, MSP means managed service provider. It’s not the healthcare or Medicare meaning of the acronym.

Why Is MSP Marketing Harder Now Than It Was Five Years Ago?

Three things happened at once. Deal sizes shrank, consolidation raised the bar for what “credible” looks like, and AI started compressing the margin on the services most MSPs sell first.

The marketing that worked when buyers had few options now has to earn attention against national brands and well-informed decision makers.

Start with deal size. In Kaseya’s data, the share of MSPs whose typical customer spends $25,000 or more per year dropped from 75% to 41% in a single year.

75% → 41%

of MSPs have a typical customer spending $25,000+ per year, in just one year. The “up to $1,000 a month” client is now the biggest band, at 30% (up from 24%).

Over the same period, the share of MSPs reporting no profit at all doubled, from 5% to 10%.

That’s a two-speed market. Providers with a defined offer and a real pipeline hold healthy margins, and a long tail competes for smaller clients at thinner spreads.

Then there’s consolidation. Omdia counted 169 publicly announced MSP acquisitions in 2025. Private equity was in 69% of the disclosed deals, and 95% of the deals happened in the same region as the acquirer.

In other words, the competitor buying up providers in your market is usually local-ish, with deep pockets.

Picture a platform-backed competitor showing up in your city. They have a national brand, a 24/7 security operations center, vertical case studies, and a marketing team.

Your prospect doesn’t know that platform bought three local shops and stapled them together. All they see is the website.

The market itself keeps growing. Analyst estimates for the global managed services market cluster somewhere between $300 billion and $440 billion, depending on what gets counted.

MSPAlliance puts the US alone north of 40,000 service providers, with 150,000 to 200,000 self-described MSPs worldwide.

Growth isn’t the problem. How that growth gets distributed is.

The last shift is the quiet one. In that same Kaseya survey, 48% of MSPs said AI and automation is now the top thing clients want from them. That’s ahead of security (42%) and backup (36%).

But only 13% said they’re turning AI into meaningful revenue.

So the AI service most MSPs haven’t packaged is becoming the first thing buyers ask about. Meanwhile, the ticket-triage work that used to fill invoices is getting automated by their own RMM vendor.

None of this makes marketing optional. It means the old version of MSP marketing, a good reputation and a phone that rings, isn’t enough anymore.

The takeaway: you can’t create demand out of thin air. What you can do is make sure your name already exists in the buyer’s head before the problem hits, so you’re the one they think of.

How Do Most MSPs Get Clients Today, and Why Does It Stop Working?

Most MSPs still grow through referrals, and referrals still close better than anything else. The problem is simple math.

A referral network gives you the same number of introductions whether you want three new clients this year or thirty. And it gives you fewer when your referrers retire or get acquired.

Good MSP lead generation turns that number into something you control instead of something that happens to you.

A Technology Marketing Toolkit survey of more than 500 MSPs found 39% doing no proactive marketing at all. They rely entirely on referrals and word of mouth.

Spend an hour in r/msp and you’ll see the same story from the inside. Owners who have never paid for a lead, never written a case study, and whose growth flattened somewhere between $1 million and $2 million in revenue.

Referrals deserve their reputation. A warm introduction from a CPA, an insurance broker, or an existing client shows up with trust already built, so the sales cycle is shorter and the close rate is higher.

The mistake is treating referrals as your whole marketing strategy instead of one channel among several. Three things break when that happens.

1. Referrals bring you more of what you already have

If your clients are 12-seat law firms, your referrals are 12-seat law firms.

The 80-seat manufacturer or the regional clinic group you want next isn’t in anyone’s address book.

2. Referral flow lags the business by years

The relationships sending you introductions today were built by an owner who was on site fixing servers in 2019.

That owner is now in meetings, and the pipeline eventually notices.

3. Referrals can’t be scheduled

You can’t decide to get four in Q2.

The fix isn’t to abandon referrals. It’s to run them like a program.

  • Ask at a defined moment. After the first quarterly business review is a good one.
  • Keep a named list of complementary partners. Accountants, lawyers, insurance brokers.
  • Give each partner a specific reason to think of you. “They do IT” isn’t specific enough.

Then build the channels that reach the decision makers nobody is going to introduce you to.

The takeaway: marketing is how you build trust, relationships, and reputation at scale, so sales cycles get shorter and more of your conversations turn into clients.

How Should an MSP Position Itself to Escape Price Competition?

You narrow who you serve, and you do the positioning work that makes the choice obvious to a buyer. If you want the full version of this, I wrote about it in how MSPs attract and convert more clients.

“IT support for small businesses” gets compared on price per user, because that’s the only visible difference between providers.

An MSP that owns a vertical, leads with compliance and security outcomes, and prices on results gives the buyer a reason to pay more and a reason to stop shopping.

Vertical specialization is the highest-return move

Most MSP marketing fails before a single channel gets chosen. It fails at the point where the owner refuses to narrow.

The evidence on niching is unusually consistent. ChannelE2E’s Top 100 Vertical Market MSPs grew collective revenue 11% in the 2024 edition.

Healthcare MSPs made up 28% of the list and financial services 18%. Healthcare was the standout, driven by compliance demands.

The editors who put the list together say the same thing every year: MSPs with a vertical focus post higher revenue and better margins than generalists.

Vendor studies put the margin premium anywhere from 10% to 30%, but none of them publish a method. I’d trust the direction and be skeptical about the exact number.

Here’s why it works. A dental practice with four locations doesn’t want “an IT services vendor.” It wants someone who already knows its practice-management software, its HIPAA exposure, and how its front desk handles imaging files.

When your pitch opens with those specifics, the conversation moves from “how much per user” to “when can you start.”

It also compounds inside your business. Same onboarding checklist, same stack, same objections, same case study told twelve times.

The trade-off is real, so let me be straight about it. Niching shrinks your addressable market and ties your risk to one sector’s economy.

Most MSPs handle that by picking two adjacent verticals with shared compliance drivers, like legal and accounting or healthcare and dental. And they keep a non-vertical service line for existing clients who don’t fit.

The takeaway: niching down is how you become the incumbent inside a segment. You own it through education and by being the default choice.

Compliance and cyber insurance do the demand generation for you

Regulation is the most reliable trigger for an MSP conversation right now.

In the US that means CMMC for defense suppliers, HIPAA for anyone touching health data, and SOC 2 for vendors selling to bigger companies.

Above all, it’s cyber insurance renewals that now demand MFA, EDR, tested backups, and documented incident response.

In the EU, NIS2 (transposed into national law from October 2024) and DORA (applied since January 17, 2025) put obligations directly on MSPs and on their clients.

Every one of these creates a dated moment where a business owner has to find a provider who can produce evidence.

It’s also the clearest example of marketing built around a pain point instead of a service list. The buyer’s problem has a deadline, and your campaign is the calendar.

An MSP positioned around that moment, with a plain-language explainer, a readiness assessment, and a named person who has walked other companies through it, isn’t competing with the generalist across town. It’s the only option the buyer found.

If security is your lane, our marketing for cybersecurity companies and MSSPs page goes deeper on this.

Move the offer from “IT support” to outcomes

The third lever is what you actually put on the price list.

Per-user and per-device pricing invites the buyer to compare line items. Outcome-based packages invite them to compare risk instead.

Think uptime and response commitments, compliance readiness, and a quarterly technology roadmap delivered by someone acting as a virtual CIO.

The delivery might be identical. The buying decision isn’t.

This is also where the AI opportunity lives. 48% of MSPs say AI is their clients’ top need, and most have nothing on the price list to sell against it.

A defined AI service is a new line item that doesn’t get compared to anybody. Think copilot rollout and governance, data-readiness assessments, or automated document workflows for one specific vertical.

What Does an Effective MSP Marketing Strategy Look Like?

It does four jobs, in order. Define a target audience narrow enough that you can be the obvious choice, build presence in that market before buyers are shopping, capture the buyers who are shopping now, and convert them with proof.

Most MSP marketing plans skip the first two jobs and then wonder why the last two underperform. If you want to put this on paper, start with my MSP marketing plan template.

Start with the buyer. B2B purchases are group decisions, even at small companies.

Forrester’s 2025 Buyers’ Journey Survey found 73% of purchases involve three or more departments. Buyers consult an average of 13 people inside their own company and nine outside it.

Scale that down to a 40-person firm and it still holds. The owner who signs, the office manager who lives with the ticket queue, the finance lead who reads the invoice, and a peer at another company whose opinion the owner trusts more than any vendor.

You have to reach all of them. And that outside voice is usually the one that decides.

The buying process is also longer than most owners expect. IT services are high-trust and high-stakes, and switching is painful.

The second finding matters more. 68% of B2B buyers already have a front-runner in mind at the very start of a purchase, and that front-runner usually wins.

~80%

is the win rate of the pre-contact favorite, according to 6sense’s 2025 Buyer Experience Report.

For an MSP, that means most of the sales process is finished before the first call. Your marketing job is to be the front-runner, which means being known in your market for months before the other provider’s contract runs out.

Most buyers are out of market at any given time. The brands that win invest in long-term demand so that when intent shows up, they’re already on the shortlist.

That split, in-market versus out-of-market, is the spine of any MSP marketing strategy. A small share of your market is actively looking this quarter. Their contract is up, their current MSP botched a migration, or an insurer sent a letter.

Those buyers search “managed IT services” plus their city, read reviews, and ask around. Everyone else isn’t looking yet, but will be, and they’ll remember whoever was useful when they weren’t.

So your marketing splits into two halves that use different channels.

1Out of market (most buyers, most of the time)

Founder-led LinkedIn, vertical content, partner relationships, community presence, and email people actually open. The test: does your name come up unprompted?

2In market (a few percent at any time)

Local SEO and Google Business Profile, paid search on high-intent terms, reviews, and a fast response. The test: do you answer in minutes, not days?

Define your ideal client profile before either half. It’s the first decision in any go-to-market plan and the one most MSPs skip.

Get specific about company size (usually 20 to 250 seats for an independent MSP), verticals, geography, the technology they run, and the compliance rules they answer to.

If you can get at it, add the pain points that make them switch. A failed restore, an insurer’s letter, or their internal IT person leaving.

An ICP written as “SMBs in the region” isn’t a profile. It’s a refusal to choose.

Everything downstream, from the sales pitch to your blog content, inherits this decision.

Which Marketing Channels Actually Generate MSP Leads?

Local SEO, a real referral and partner program, founder-led LinkedIn, and vertical content. Those are the ones that produce qualified leads for most MSPs.

Paid search works when you model it against client lifetime value instead of lead count. Cold outbound and direct mail still work, at lower rates.

Most MSPs need three channels run well, not ten run at 10%.

A quick test for busy work: if you can’t link an activity to a company goal, if you report on vanity metrics, or if you’re running twenty things at ten percent each, that’s busy work. Do five things well. Depth is what compounds.

Local SEO and Google Business Profile

If you sell inside a metro area, local SEO is the highest-intent organic channel you have.

“Managed IT services Denver,” “IT support near me,” and “cybersecurity company” plus a city are typed by people who’ve already decided to buy something.

The local pack and the top organic results take most of that traffic. Anything below the fold on page one gets a fraction of it.

Here’s what a strong presence looks like in practice:

  • A Google Business Profile that’s claimed, categorized correctly, filled with services, photographed, and reviewed at a steady pace.
  • Location pages for every city you serve. Real pages with local proof, not one template with the city name swapped.
  • Consistent name, address, and phone number across directories.
  • Service pages built around the terms buyers use, not the terms vendors use.

Link building matters less for local terms than for national ones. But a handful of links from local business associations, partners, and chambers of commerce moves the needle in smaller markets, and it’s the cheapest link building you’ll ever do.

Content marketing and thought leadership

MSP content marketing has a bad reputation because most of it is vendor content with a logo swapped on. “5 signs you need managed services,” published as a blog post nobody links to.

Content that works does one of three things:

  • It answers the specific questions your vertical asks. What does a HIPAA risk assessment involve? What will the insurer ask for at renewal?
  • It shows your expertise in your own words.
  • It gives people something they can use before they buy, like a checklist, a readiness scorecard, or a plain-language explainer for their board.

Publish less, and make it count. Ten pieces that a prospect in your vertical would forward to a colleague beat a hundred posts written for a keyword tool.

If you want more on this, take a look at our content marketing for MSPs page. There’s also an MSP-specific bonus: this is how you get cited by AI assistants, which I’ll cover later in this guide.

Founder-led LinkedIn

Your product is trust, so your own LinkedIn is probably the most under-used asset in your marketing.

Your buyer is a business owner or operator, and they’re on LinkedIn. They’ll hire the person who explained, in plain terms, why their last IT vendor’s backup “worked” until the day it needed to restore.

The format that works is operator content. What you saw this week, what went wrong, what you’d tell a peer.

It reads as expertise because it is expertise. And it lets the buyer see how you think before they’ve ever spoken to you.

Three to five posts a week from you, in your voice, with comments answered, will beat any amount of company-page posting.

Pair that with deliberate engagement. Connect with the people you’d want as clients, comment on what they post, and let the content do the introduction.

Company pages still have a role. They back up the founder, host the case studies, and give employees something to share. They just don’t generate demand on their own.

Paid search and paid social

Google Ads for managed services is expensive, and it works as a lead generation channel. In that order.

WordStream’s 2026 benchmarks, drawn from more than 13,000 US search campaigns, put the average cost per click at $5.42. That’s up from $5.26 the year before.

Business services came in at $5.87 per click with a cost per lead of about $94, well above the $67 average across all industries.

And those are medians across all kinds of businesses. IT services terms in competitive metros run well above that.

So the channel only makes sense if you model it against client value. Here’s a worked example.

A 50-seat client at $100 per user per month is $5,000 a month in revenue. At a 40% gross margin, that’s $2,000 a month in contribution.

$48K

of contribution over two years from that one 50-seat client. Over five years, it’s $120,000.

Against that, a cost per closed client in the low thousands is fine. Against a lead count target, the same spend looks like throwing money at a wall.

Judge the channel on quality leads that close, and it earns its place.

Paid social on LinkedIn works better for out-of-market work than for lead capture. Put your content and a vertical asset in front of a named list.

Meta ads occasionally work for local retargeting and very small businesses, but they rarely produce the 50-seat client.

Paid ads should be the last channel you turn on, after positioning, your website, and local SEO are in place. Ads amplify whatever your site already does, so if the site doesn’t convert, ads just make that failure faster and more expensive.

Email nurture

Email is the owned channel that escapes algorithms.

A monthly note from you to everyone who’s ever asked a question, with one useful thing in it, keeps you in the room while the prospect is out of market. The insurance renewal checklist, the new phishing pattern hitting law firms, the CMMC deadline.

Segmented campaigns beat newsletters sent to everyone. The vertical list gets the vertical content.

Follow-up sequences after a download or an assessment request should be short, specific, and human. What the assessment covers, what to expect, one relevant case study, and a calendar link.

You nurture leads by being useful, not frequent. Automation helps with timing and segmentation, but it doesn’t create qualified leads on its own.

Partner networks

The referral program from earlier belongs on this list because it’s a real lead channel, and most MSPs under-manage it.

Your best partners are the advisers a business owner already trusts. Accountants, business lawyers, insurance brokers (especially cyber insurance brokers, who now have a regulatory reason to want a competent MSP on speed dial), commercial real estate agents, and vendors that serve the same vertical.

Run it like marketing. A named list, a reason to refer that’s specific to each partner, a shared piece of content, a quarterly touch, and a thank-you that’s more than a gift card.

Co-hosted events work well here. A lunch-and-learn on cyber insurance with the broker, or a compliance briefing with the law firm, will generate more introductions in a morning than a year of “let me know if you hear of anyone.”

Done consistently, a partner program produces a steady flow of warm introductions that no other channel matches on close rate.

Reviews, directories, and proof

Reviews close deals the buyer has already half-decided on.

A Google Business Profile with 60 recent, specific reviews beats one with eight generic ones. And specifics matter more than the count.

“They migrated our 3 clinics to the cloud over a weekend with no downtime” is basically a case study in one sentence.

Ask at the moment of relief, after a successful project or a fast resolution. Clutch and G2 matter if you sell to larger companies, but for local SMB buyers, Google is the review site.

Case studies and video testimonials are the same asset for a different stage. Two or three strong ones per vertical, on your site and in your sales process, are worth more than twenty thin ones.

Events and lunch-and-learns

In-person still works when you sell trust locally.

Small beats big. A breakfast briefing for 15 practice managers on what their insurer will ask this year will do more than a booth at a regional expo.

Vendor events like IT Nation, Kaseya Connect, and Channel Partners (and the Canalys and Omdia forums in Europe) are for learning and partnerships. They’re not for finding clients.

Cold outbound

Cold calling, cold email, and direct mail built a lot of MSPs, and they still bring in deals.

But close rates are low, buyer tolerance is lower, and the effort competes for your time with everything above.

Outsourced lead generation and appointment setting for MSPs tends to deliver meetings that look like leads and behave like cold calls.

If you use outbound, keep it targeted. A named list in one vertical, one trigger, one offer. And pair it with the out-of-market work so the person you’re contacting has heard of you.

Account-based marketing, for the few MSPs it fits

Account-based marketing makes sense if you’re selling into 100-seat-plus companies with multiple decision makers and $10,000-plus monthly contracts.

There, a named-account program with personalized outreach and executive content can pay for itself with a handful of wins.

Below that size, the same discipline applied loosely is enough. Keep a short list of dream accounts and go after them on purpose.

How Much Should an MSP Spend on Marketing?

If you’re in growth mode, plan on roughly 5% to 10% of revenue, including your own time. Lower end if you’re growing mostly on referrals, upper end if you’re building new channels.

But set the actual budget from a payback calculation on client lifetime value, not from a percentage alone.

Cross-industry benchmarks give you a floor. Gartner’s CMO Spend Survey put average marketing budgets at 7.7% of revenue in 2025 and 7.8% in 2026.

Keep in mind its panel skews toward companies above $1 billion in revenue, and smaller companies consistently spend a higher share. The Deloitte/Duke CMO Survey, with a broader panel, reports 9.4%.

Most independent MSPs spend well below either number and treat the owner’s time as the unbudgeted line item. That’s a big reason the referral ceiling arrives so predictably. When your time runs out, so does the marketing.

A better way to size the budget is to work backward from what a client is worth and how long you keep them.

Take the same 50-seat client at $100 per user per month. That’s $5,000 in monthly revenue and, at a 40% gross margin, $2,000 a month in contribution.

Keep that client for five years and they’re worth $120,000 in margin.

Spending three to four months of revenue, so $15,000 to $20,000, to win that client is a comfortable payback. Spending that on a $1,000-a-month client isn’t.

That’s the arithmetic sustainable growth comes from, not a percentage copied from a benchmark.

And it follows your ICP. If you’ve decided to go after larger, stickier clients, you can afford to spend more to win them.

Two budget lines get missed all the time.

The first is vendor co-marketing money. Microsoft, Kaseya, ConnectWise, N-able, Pax8, and the distributors all run market development funds (MDF) and partner marketing programs, often as 50:50 cost-shares.

Partner marketing teams say a large share of it goes unclaimed, because smaller partners don’t have the time to put together a compliant proposal.

MDF is free money for campaigns you were already going to run. It’s not a strategy, though, and the co-branded content it produces rarely stands out.

Claim it, and use it to fund the vertical content and events you designed yourself.

The second is measurement. Attribution for a business with a nine-month sales cycle and a referral-heavy funnel is imperfect by nature.

Pretend otherwise, and you’ll end up cutting the channels that create demand in favor of the ones that just capture it.

What works for MSPs is simple:

  • A CRM source field that sales actually fills in.
  • A “how did you hear about us” question on every form.
  • An honest quarterly review of where closed deals started.

How Do You Build an MSP Marketing Plan by Revenue Stage?

Match the plan to your stage, because a $700,000 MSP and a $7 million MSP have different constraints, different buyers, and different owners.

Think of these as a sequence, not a menu. Each stage assumes the one before it is in place.

If you want a fill-in version, here’s my MSP marketing plan template, and for the longer view, my 3-year MSP growth roadmap.

Under $1 million: pick a lane and fix the basics

At this stage you are the marketing department, so the plan has to fit in a few hours a week.

  1. Choose one vertical or one compliance driver. Rewrite the homepage, the services page, and the Google Business Profile around it. Generalist positioning at this size is a decision to compete on price.
  2. Fix the in-market layer. Claim and complete the Google Business Profile, get to 25 specific reviews, build one real location page, and make sure a form submission reaches a human within ten minutes during business hours.
  3. Systematize referrals. A written ask, a moment to ask it, and a list of five partners you talk to every quarter.
  4. Start LinkedIn. Two posts a week, in your own voice.

Skip paid ads, skip the podcast, and skip the newsletter until you have a list.

The goal here is to become the obvious choice for one kind of business in one place.

$1 million to $5 million: build the engine

The referral ceiling shows up in this band. The plan is to add lead generation channels that reach buyers no one is going to introduce you to.

  1. A content and local SEO engine. Vertical service pages, one substantial piece a month aimed at the questions your vertical asks, location pages for every metro you serve, and a review cadence tied to your ticket system.
  2. LinkedIn at full cadence. Three to five posts a week, plus a company page that carries the case studies.
  3. A formal partner program. Three to five complementary advisers, a co-hosted event each quarter, and a shared piece of content.
  4. A paid search test. Google Ads on your five highest-intent local terms, run for 90 days against a cost-per-closed-client target, not a cost-per-lead target.
  5. A first marketing hire or an agency. Either way, you stay the face and the marketing function runs the system. This is also where the CRM has to become the source of truth.
  6. An AI offer. Whatever your vertical asks for first, like copilot governance or document automation, turn it into a package and put it on your site. Half the industry says clients are asking for it, and 13% are selling it.

$5 million to $20 million and beyond: own the category

By now you’re competing with platform-backed providers, so the plan is about authority.

  1. A dedicated marketing lead who owns pipeline, not just content.
  2. Vertical thought leadership and earned media. Original research, speaking at MSP events and your vertical’s events, trade press contributions, and podcast appearances. This is also what gets you cited by AI assistants, which increasingly shape the shortlist.
  3. Account-based marketing on a named list of 50 to 100 dream accounts, with executive content and coordinated outreach.
  4. Vendor co-marketing at scale, using MDF to fund the vertical programs you designed in-house.
  5. A sales and marketing operating rhythm. Shared definitions of a qualified lead, a weekly pipeline review, and attribution both teams believe. This is where most MSPs of this size leak revenue, because sales was built on referrals, marketing was built on leads, and neither trusts the other’s numbers.

How Does MSP Marketing Differ Between the US and Europe?

The strategies are the same. The triggers, the proof, and the calendar are different.

In the US, MSP marketing is driven by cyber insurance, vertical compliance frameworks, and a mature local search market.

In Europe, it’s driven by regulation that names MSPs directly, buyers who research longer and trust vendors less, and language markets that fragment every channel.

Regulation is the European advantage

NIS2 lists managed service providers and managed security service providers in Annex I of its high-criticality sectors.

That brings any MSP with 50 or more employees or €10 million in turnover into scope as a regulated entity in its own right. It also obliges thousands of their mid-sized clients to prove their supply chain is secure.

DORA does the same for financial entities and their ICT providers.

An MSP in the Netherlands, Germany, or the Nordics has a compliance conversation to open with almost every prospect in scope. And the proof it needs is documentation, not marketing copy.

GDPR shapes outbound too. Cold email to individuals is legally narrower than in the US, which pushes European MSP marketing further toward inbound, partner, and event channels.

The buyer is more skeptical and the cycle is longer

European SMB owners tend to research more, take fewer vendor calls, and put more weight on peer references.

So founder-led content and partner introductions carry more of the load, and paid search carries less.

Language fragments everything

A Benelux MSP serving Dutch, French, and English-speaking clients needs localized service pages, reviews, and content for each. And the search volumes in each language are a fraction of the US figures.

That makes ranking easier and scaling harder. The winners tend to dominate one language market before moving into the next.

Events and communities differ

The US calendar runs through IT Nation, Kaseya Connect, Channel Partners, and the MSP Summit.

In Europe, Canalys and Omdia forums, national IT associations, and the UK MSP community matter more. The UK community is unusually well-organized, with its own peer groups and coaches.

In both regions, the events that produce clients are the small local ones you host, not the big ones you attend.

The US has denser competition and a bigger PE presence

With tens of thousands of providers and heavy platform acquisition activity, US metros are some of the most crowded MSP markets in the world. Differentiation isn’t optional there.

In most European cities, a well-positioned vertical specialist still faces a handful of credible providers, not dozens.

How Is AI Changing MSP Marketing?

In three directions at once. It’s a new service to sell, it’s compressing the margin on the services MSPs used to sell first, and it’s changing how buyers research providers.

The MSPs that come out ahead will market the first, reprice around the second, and rebuild their content for the third.

AI as the offer

The demand is on the table. 48% of MSPs say AI and automation is their clients’ top need, ahead of security.

The supply isn’t there. Only 13% report meaningful AI revenue.

A packaged, vertical-specific AI service is the rare case of a marketing message the buyer already wrote for you. Think governance and rollout of copilots, data readiness, or automated intake and documentation workflows.

AI as the threat

The managed services that filled the first page of every MSP proposal are getting automated. Tier-1 ticket triage, password resets, and routine monitoring, by the same RMM and PSA vendors MSPs pay every month.

The revenue doesn’t vanish, but the labor margin does.

That pushes your value toward the things software can’t do. Judgment, accountability, the relationship, and the roadmap.

Your marketing has to say that out loud. If you’re still positioning on “24/7 helpdesk,” you’re advertising the part of your business that’s getting cheaper.

AI as the research tool

Buyers now ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google’s AI Overviews for shortlists.

Forrester’s State of Business Buying, 2026 describes generative AI search as the starting point of the B2B buying process. It also finds that buyers, wary of incomplete answers, then check what the assistant told them with people and sources they trust.

For the kinds of searches an MSP cares about, AI Overviews already show up on most long-tail queries. And the sources they cite are often Reddit threads, YouTube videos, and vendor glossary pages, not MSP websites.

So what gets cited? Content that’s structured (a direct answer under a question heading, a table, a stat with a source), specific to a vertical or a city, and backed up elsewhere through reviews, directories, partner mentions, and press.

That’s generative engine optimization in practice, and it’s mostly good content marketing with clearer structure.

Your website doesn’t become obsolete in this world. It becomes the place buyers go to check what the assistant told them, which raises the bar for what the site has to prove.

The practical version is simple:

  • Answer your vertical’s questions plainly on your site.
  • Keep your Google Business Profile and directory listings consistent.
  • Get named in the places assistants read, like local press, association pages, and partner sites.
  • Make sure your public content says the same thing your website does.

What Are the Most Common MSP Marketing Mistakes?

Relying on referrals alone, marketing to everyone, running random tactics with no system, under-investing until growth stalls, and measuring activity instead of pipeline.

Every one of them has a straightforward fix. Most MSPs make at least three at once. For more, see my post on common MSP SEO mistakes and six ways to improve your MSP marketing.

1Referral dependence

Covered above. The fix is a referral program plus channels that reach strangers.

2No niche

“IT support for businesses of all sizes” is a price list, not a position.

✕Too broad

“IT support for businesses of all sizes.”

✓Specific

“Managed IT and HIPAA compliance for dental groups.”

3Random acts of marketing

A webinar in March, a mailer in June, a LinkedIn burst in September. Each one runs once, nothing gets measured, and nothing gets learned.

The fix: pick three channels and review them every quarter.

4Vendor content with your logo on it

Buyers can tell.

Write your own, about your vertical’s pain points, in your voice.

5A website that describes services and hides everything else

No pricing signals, no named people, no case studies, and no answer to what happens after the contract is signed.

Buyers want to know exactly what tomorrow looks like if they say yes. A site that leaves them guessing loses to one that shows the team, the onboarding calendar, and the first ninety days.

6Slow response to inbound

A form that gets answered the next morning is a lead handed to the competitor who answered in four minutes.

And if you don’t share a definition of a qualified lead, you’ll keep answering the wrong ones first.

7Never asking for the sale, the review, or the referral

Most MSP owners are engineers by temperament, and engineers hate asking.

Build the ask into the process so nobody has to improvise it.

8Measuring the wrong thing

Impressions, followers, and website traffic are inputs. Pipeline created, cost per closed client, and win rate are outcomes.

A busy marketing function with no pipeline isn’t a success. It’s a diagnosis.

9Under-investing, then quitting early

Local SEO takes six to nine months. Content takes about a year. LinkedIn takes a hundred posts.

If you fund a channel for a quarter and cancel it, you’ve paid for the expensive part and skipped the return.

10Positioning on the part of the business that’s getting automated

Leading with helpdesk response times today advertises exactly the service AI is making cheaper.

The pattern behind all ten: lots of activity with no pipeline impact is the sign of scattered marketing. Tie every activity to the buyer journey and the pipeline, then realign your ICP, positioning, and channels around what actually moves deals.

Should an MSP Hire a Marketing Agency or Build In-House?

Build in-house when you’ll be the face of the company and revenue can fund one competent generalist.

Hire an agency when the engine has to be built faster than one hire can build it, or when your time is the bottleneck.

Either way, keep strategy in-house. Agencies run channels. They shouldn’t decide who you’re for.

The MSP marketing agency market is crowded and uneven. It includes specialists who’ve only worked in the MSP space for a decade, generalists who add “MSP” to a landing page, appointment-setting shops calling themselves marketing, and coaching programs that sell peer groups and templates.

Prices range from a few hundred dollars a month for templated content to five figures a month for a full retained team.

Here are the questions that separate a good one from the rest:

  • Which of their MSP clients grew, by how much, and over what period?
  • Who will actually do the work, and how senior are they?
  • What happens to your content and data if you leave?
  • Will they tell you when a channel isn’t working for you?
  • Have they ever sold to a business owner who buys IT?

The in-house route is cheaper per hour and slower to compound. A single marketing hire at a $2 million MSP will spend the first six months on the website, the Google Business Profile, and the CRM before any channel is running properly.

That’s fine if you’re patient and the referral flow holds. It’s expensive if the referral ceiling has already hit.

The hybrid most MSPs land on is an internal owner of marketing (often you plus a coordinator), specialist help for the technical channels like local SEO, paid search, and design, and your voice kept entirely in-house.

Whichever model you pick, whoever runs it should be able to say in one sentence which three channels you’re building, why, and how you’ll measure them.

FAQ

What does MSP stand for in marketing?

MSP stands for managed service provider. That’s a company that manages a client’s IT, security, or cloud environment for a recurring fee.

“MSP marketing” is the marketing those providers do to win clients. The acronym also shows up in healthcare and Medicare administration with unrelated meanings.

How do MSPs get clients?

Most get them through referrals from existing clients and trusted advisers like accountants, lawyers, and insurance brokers.

As they grow, the successful ones add local search, founder-led LinkedIn, vertical content, partner programs, and, selectively, paid search. That way new clients show up from outside their existing network.

What is the best marketing channel for an MSP?

For an independent MSP under $5 million, the best combination is a systematic referral and partner program, local SEO with a maintained Google Business Profile, and the founder’s LinkedIn.

Together they cover warm introductions, active local buyers, and the buyers who’ll be shopping next year.

How much should an MSP spend on marketing?

Between 5% and 10% of revenue is a reasonable range in growth mode, including your own time.

A better method is to set a cost-per-closed-client target from client lifetime value. Three to four months of a client’s monthly revenue is a comfortable acquisition cost for someone you expect to keep five years.

Do referrals still work for MSPs in 2027?

Yes, and they still close at the highest rate of any source.

They stop being enough, rather than stop working, somewhere between $1 million and $2 million in revenue. That’s when the network produces fewer introductions than your growth plan needs.

What are the most effective MSP marketing strategies?

A narrow position (vertical or compliance-led) plus three channels run consistently. A referral and partner program, local SEO, and founder-led LinkedIn, with email to stay in front of buyers who aren’t ready yet.

It’s less about which tactics you pick and more about doing a few of them well for long enough.

Should an MSP hire an MSP marketing agency?

It depends on whether your time or your missing skills are the constraint.

An agency makes sense when the engine has to be built faster than one hire can build it. In-house makes sense when you’ll be the face and can fund one competent generalist. Either way, keep positioning and strategy in-house.

Should an MSP specialize in a vertical?

Almost always.

Vertical MSPs report higher margins and pricing premiums, close faster because they speak the buyer’s language, and run more efficiently. The risk is sector concentration, which most specialists manage by choosing two adjacent verticals with shared compliance drivers.

Does cold calling still work for MSPs?

It works at low rates and with a real cost to your brand.

It’s most effective when it’s tightly targeted, with one vertical and one trigger like an insurance renewal or a regulatory deadline, and when the prospect has already heard your name before the call.

How long does MSP marketing take to work?

Referral programs and paid search produce results within weeks. Local SEO takes three to nine months, founder-led LinkedIn three to six, and vertical content six to twelve.

If you fund a channel for less than two quarters, you’ll rarely see the return.

Should an MSP use vendor MDF and co-marketing?

Yes, to fund campaigns you designed in-house.

Market development funds from Microsoft, Kaseya, ConnectWise, N-able, Pax8, and the distributors go widely unclaimed and typically match 50:50. The co-branded templates that come with them won’t help you stand out, so take the money and skip the message.

How is AI affecting MSP marketing?

In three ways. Clients want AI services and few MSPs sell them, AI is compressing the margin on tier-1 support (which changes what you should lead with), and buyers use AI assistants to research providers.

That last one rewards structured, specific content that’s backed up elsewhere online, which is what gets cited.
